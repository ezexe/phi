The register model

phi_prune.register holds two registers side by side, a Zeckendorf register and a binary register. Each is a list of digits, least significant first, that local rewrite rules bring to its normal form. The module runs phi-prune's own multiply–accumulate step in each, so the two can be measured on the same sums.

  • Place values.The Zeckendorf register weighs its digits 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, …, the place values FibonacciEncoder uses. The binary register weighs them 1, 2, 4, 8, ….
  • Normal form.Every digit is 0 or 1. In the Zeckendorf register no two adjacent digits are both 1, so its normal form is the Zeckendorf representation.
  • Local rules.Each rule keeps the register's value and touches at most three nearby digits. Since every value has one normal form, rules can fire in any order and every order ends at that normal form.

Add two numbers in both registers

This runs the module's rules in the page: from_int writes each number in normal form, digit-wise addition adds the two registers digit by digit with no carries, and each press of Next round fires one normalization round as normalize_rounds does, every rule that can fire with no two rules on the same digit, from the lowest position up. Digits read most significant on the left. Once a register is in normal form, click one of its digits to flip it.

The rules

A digit may exceed 1 for a while, as it does right after two registers are added digit by digit. These rules bring it back, and each keeps the value:

Here F(k) is the place value of digit k, so F(0) = 1 and F(1) = 2. At the bottom of the register the removal of 2 digits has nowhere two places down to go: at k = 1 it is 2 + 2 = 3 + 1, into d[2] and d[0], and at k = 0 it is 1 + 1 = 2, into d[1] alone.

A Zeckendorf carry also moves down, two places, while a binary carry only moves up. test_phi_carry_runs_down_binary_does_not holds both: apply([0, 0, 2], "split", 2) gives [1, 0, 0, 1], which is 2 · 3 = 5 + 1, and the binary carry gives [0, 0, 0, 1].

apply refuses a rule that cannot fire, at a position outside the register or on a digit too small to take from, so no digit ever goes below 0. rewrites lists every rule that can fire, and normalize fires them until none can, the lowest position first, or one at random with an rng, which stands in for self-timed hardware where any local rewrite may happen first.

Confluence

Both rule sets are confluent: every order of firing reaches the same normal form, because each rule keeps the value and each value has one normal form. Neither register needs a clock to decide the order. tests/test_register.py checks this on 200 random registers, each brought to normal form in 10 random orders, and checks that every rule keeps the value on 500 random registers with digits from 0 to 3. test_add_exhaustive adds every pair of 6-digit registers in normal form of each kind and checks the sum and its normal form.

How many digits a register needs

An n-digit string with no two adjacent 1s ends either in 0, after any such string of n − 1 digits, or in 01, after any such string of n − 2 digits. So the count Z(n) of such strings is Z(n − 1) + Z(n − 2), with Z(0) = 1 and Z(1) = 2, which gives 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, …, the register's own place values. In the standard numbering of the Fibonacci numbers, F(1) = F(2) = 1, Z(n) is F(n + 2), and by Binet's formula F(n + 2) is the nearest integer to φn+2/√5, which is about 1.171 · φn, with φ = 1.618…. Each Zeckendorf digit therefore carries log2 φ, about 0.694 bits, so holding a given number of levels takes about 1.44 times as many digits as binary.

Normalization rounds

normalize_rounds counts time instead of work. Each normalization round fires at once every rule it can without two rules touching the same digit, picked from the lowest position up, and the count of rounds stands in for the time a register takes to reach normal form when every cell rewrites itself in parallel. A self-timed register stops after the rounds a sum needs. A clocked register waits the worst case on every sum. The register's completion signal is a local scan: a register still on its way holds a digit above 1 or, in the Zeckendorf register, two adjacent 1s, and one in normal form holds neither.

That ratio compares a clocked binary register with a self-timed Zeckendorf one. A self-timed binary register reaches normal form sooner than the Zeckendorf register on every row.

Single-bit errors

Every binary digit pattern is a valid number, so a flipped bit in a binary register cannot be seen. A Zeckendorf register in normal form holds no two adjacent 1s, so a flip from 0 to 1 beside a 1 leaves a pattern that cannot be in normal form, and flip_detected reads it as corrupt. A flip from 1 to 0 always leaves a valid pattern and goes unseen.

correction_candidates lists the registers in normal form one change from 1 to 0 away from a corrupt one. A flip that lands between two 1s, 101 to 111, has one candidate and can be corrected. A lone 11 could have come from either cell, so it has two and can only be detected.

Zeckendorf normalization does not undo a flip. The rules keep the corrupted value and bring it to normal form, so a register brought to normal form after a flip is worth the original plus the flipped place value. test_normalizing_keeps_the_corrupted_value checks this for every detected flip of every 8-digit register, so the scan has to run before normalization.

Multiply–accumulate in a register

dot(levels, xs, kind) takes non-negative integer weight levels and activations and accumulates their dot product in one register. Each weight level is written in the register's own digits, and each of its digits that is 1 adds a partial product, the activation times that place value, to the accumulator by digit-wise addition, after which normalize_rounds brings the accumulator to normal form. A level's Hamming weight is therefore its number of additions. In binary a partial product x · 2k is a shift, wiring only. In a Zeckendorf register x · F(k) is a Fibonacci multiple of x, which dot takes as given and writes with from_int. Built in hardware, the multiples take additions, x · F(k) = x · F(k − 1) + x · F(k − 2), once per activation and shared by every weight it meets, and the notebook below counts their rounds with ladder_rounds. test_dot checks the exact sum and one addition for each digit that is 1 in each weight whose activation is not zero.

The Jupyter notebook notebooks/register_settling.ipynb in ezexe/p4-phi-experiments runs dot on one φ-pruned convolution layer, ResNet-18's layer3.0.conv1 with 256 output channels, 128 kept, and a fan-in of 1152, quantized per output channel. Its saved run used its fallback, Laplace-distributed synthetic weights of that shape and half-normal activations, since torchvision was not installed where it ran. It took 32 dot products, 8 kept channels by 4 patches, and counts rounds per dot product four ways: the Zeckendorf register self-timed, with the rounds of its Fibonacci multiples spread over the kept channels; a self-timed binary register; a clocked binary ripple-carry adder, which waits the accumulator's width on every addition, the bits of the largest sum the layer's fan-in can reach: 23 at 6 digits, 24 at 8 and 26 at 10; and a clocked carry-lookahead adder, which waits the base-2 logarithm of that width rounded up, 5 at each of the three.

A pre-registered check on a small transformer

scripts/transformer_check.py in ezexe/p4-phi-experiments tests φ-pruning and the Zeckendorf representation of quantized weights on a character-level GPT with 4 layers, width 128 and MLP width 512, which learns the source of the standard library of the Python that runs it, on CPU. Per seed it trains dense for 1500 steps, prunes MLP hidden neurons one-shot and fine-tunes 300 steps with each mask fixed, and quantizes every Linear weight per output channel. The metric is bits per character on held-out files, lower being better. Its kill criteria are in its docstring, written before any run. Each result file is the record of one three-seed run, and a run on another Python trains on other text and gives other figures.

Uniform affine levels are round((w − min) · scale) per output channel, computed by the script's own quantize_levels on the formula of the package's encode_tensor, except that its torch.round rounds ties to even where encode_tensor rounds them up. The delta of a fine-tuned model

--part delta trains a base model per seed on most of the training files, fine-tunes a copy for 300 steps on another tenth of them, and takes the delta of every Linear layer, the fine-tuned minus the base weights. Over the three seeds the general validation went from 2.1163 to 2.0165 bits per character, the held-out part of the fine-tuning files went from 1.6445 to 1.7124, and the delta's norm is 0.1459 of the weights' norm, so the fine-tune behaved as continued training of an undertrained base. Weights and deltas are both quantized symmetrically per output channel:

Per-channel quantization divides each channel by its own largest value, so it sees only the shape of a spread, and the delta's spread has the shape of the weights'.

The same run codes the positions of the delta's largest entries with Fibonacci-coded gaps, Elias gamma, Elias delta and a fixed-length code. That part is on the Fibonacci codes page, beside phi_prune.delta, which writes a delta's kept positions as the same Fibonacci-coded gaps in a .phid file.

What the measurements show

  • Fewer additions at low precision. On trained weights, Zeckendorf levels have a lower Hamming weight than binary and CSD from 16 to 55 uniform affine levels and through 377 levels in sign-magnitude form, and on the delta, quantized symmetrically per channel, at the 21 and 55 levels measured, so a multiply by adding Fibonacci multiples takes fewer additions there. On trained weights CSD has the lower count above that.
  • Self-timed against clocked. A self-timed Zeckendorf accumulator takes fewer rounds per dot product than a clocked binary one, and more than a self-timed binary one, whose additions reach normal form in fewer rounds.
  • Single-bit error detection on one side only. About two in five single-bit flips of a Zeckendorf register in normal form are detected and under one in ten can be corrected, while a binary register detects none; normalization keeps a corrupted value, so the check comes first.
  • φ-pruning on a transformer's MLP. At 50% it is worse than 2 of every 4 in every seed, and reordering closes the gap only by turning it into the heaviest half; at 25% and 75% φ-pruning, the matching pattern of every 4 and the heaviest neurons reach the same loss after fine-tuning, and a random three quarters is worse in every seed.
  • Scale. The transformer has 4 layers of width 128 and trains 1500 steps on CPU, and the register figures count rewrite rounds, so the results give directions and not sizes.

python -m pytest tests/test_register.py
